\section{Abstract}
% ============================================================================
Timely diagnosis of crop diseases is a high-leverage intervention for
smallholder farmers, yet expert diagnostic knowledge rarely reaches the field
in time. This project builds a bilingual (Hindi/English) web
application that classifies a photographed leaf, assesses severity, and
generates a location- and weather-aware treatment plan with spoken audio
output. The classifier was trained and evaluated on a 15-class subset of the
PlantVillage dataset (Pepper, Potato, Tomato; 20{,}624 images) under a
stratified, leakage-checked 70/15/15 split, comparing a VGG-style CNN trained
from scratch against a fine-tuned EfficientNetB3 transfer-learning model. On
a held-out set of 3{,}094 images, the from-scratch CNN reached 95.51\%
accuracy (94.59\% macro-F1) and was selected for deployment, outperforming
EfficientNetB3 (87.10\%) while also being better calibrated (Expected
Calibration Error 0.031), and was exported to a quantized on-device format
with verified prediction fidelity. Around this classifier, the application
adds a facts-based severity engine, a language-model-driven treatment planner
conditioned on location and live weather, and text-to-speech narration. The
report states plainly what the evaluation itself reveals: PlantVillage is a
lab-collected, uniform-background benchmark, and published work shows such
models can lose accuracy on field-collected photographs -- a limitation
discussed openly, with concrete steps to close the gap.

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================
Plant diseases are estimated to destroy a large share of global crop yield
every season, and the interval between a disease first appearing on a leaf
and a farmer reaching expert diagnosis is often what separates a controllable
outbreak from a lost harvest. The spread of low-cost smartphone cameras has
made a different intervention practical: image-based diagnosis that returns a
preliminary result in seconds rather than days. Mohanty et al.\ (2016)
\cite{mohanty2016} showed that convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on
a large public leaf-image dataset could classify 26 diseases across 14 crop
species with over 99\% accuracy on held-out images from that same dataset --
a result this project's own evaluation approaches in scale (Section~6), and
whose central caveat, that laboratory images are not field images, this
report also inherits and discusses honestly (Section~7).

A diagnosis alone is of limited practical use without two further pieces of
information: how urgently the farmer must act, and what, specifically, to do
about it, in their own region, under current conditions. Most published
disease-classification work stops at the first question. This project treats
classification as one stage of a larger decision-support pipeline that also
answers the second question -- severity and a concrete, localized,
weather-aware treatment recommendation -- delivered in a language and format
usable by a non-technical user in the field, including as spoken audio.

\subsection{Objectives}
The main objective is to design, train, evaluate, and deploy a deep-learning
crop-disease diagnosis system that couples an image classifier with severity
scoring and a localized, weather-aware treatment-recommendation engine,
delivered through a bilingual web interface with audio output. The specific
sub-objectives are to: (i)~curate and statistically characterize a labeled
leaf-image dataset; (ii)~design, train, and rigorously compare two CNN
architectures under an identical, leakage-free evaluation protocol;
(iii)~evaluate the models beyond raw accuracy, including calibration and
explainability; (iv)~export the selected model to a quantized,
deployment-ready format and verify that quantization does not silently
degrade accuracy; and (v)~build the severity, treatment, and
speech-narration layers around the classifier.

\subsection{Scope}
The classification scope of this report is the 15-class Pepper/Potato/Tomato
subset of PlantVillage described in Section~4.2; this is the dataset and set
of classes referred to throughout Sections~4-6. The application scope covers
the full farmer-facing workflow: image upload, diagnosis, severity, location
capture, weather context, treatment-plan generation, and audio playback.
Field-image generalization, multi-disease detection, and a native mobile
client are treated as future work (Section~7.5), not as part of the delivered
system.

\section{Background (Literature Review)}
% ============================================================================
Modern image-based disease recognition rests on the broader progress of deep
convolutional networks for visual classification. Krizhevsky et al.\ (2012)
\cite{krizhevsky2012} demonstrated with AlexNet that a deep CNN trained on
large-scale image data could substantially outperform prior hand-engineered
feature pipelines, which triggered a sequence of deeper architectures:
Simonyan and Zisserman (2015) \cite{simonyan2015} showed that stacking many
small $3\times3$ convolutional filters (the VGG family) improved accuracy
with a conceptually simple, uniform design -- the design this project's
custom CNN (Section~4.5.1) is directly modeled on -- while He et al.\ (2016)
\cite{he2016resnet} introduced residual connections to make substantially
deeper networks trainable. Tan and Le (2019) \cite{tan2019efficientnet}
later proposed EfficientNet, which scales network depth, width, and input
resolution jointly under a fixed compute budget; this project uses its B3
variant as a transfer-learning baseline (Section~4.5.2).

Within agriculture specifically, Hughes and Salathé (2015)
\cite{hughes2015} released the PlantVillage image repository -- over 50{,}000
expert-curated leaf images spanning many crops and diseases -- explicitly to
enable mobile disease-diagnostic tools, and it has since become the
dominant benchmark in this sub-field. Building on it, Mohanty et al.\ (2016)
\cite{mohanty2016} trained CNNs on 54{,}306 PlantVillage images (14 species,
26 diseases) and reported 99.35\% held-out accuracy, while Ferentinos (2018)
\cite{ferentinos2018}, training on a larger 87{,}848-image, 58-class corpus,
reported 99.53\%. Too et al.\ (2019) \cite{too2019} compared fine-tuned VGG16,
Inception-V4, several depths of ResNet, and DenseNet121 on the same 38-class
PlantVillage split used by most of this literature, finding DenseNet121 best
at 99.75\% with the least evidence of overfitting -- directly motivating why
this project also compares a from-scratch architecture against a
transfer-learning one rather than assuming either is superior a priori
(Section~6.2 revisits this comparison for the present dataset).

Because PlantVillage images are collected under controlled, laboratory
conditions, a second and smaller line of work has trained on field-collected
photographs instead. Ramcharan et al.\ (2017) \cite{ramcharan2017} applied
transfer learning to cassava leaf images taken directly in Tanzanian fields,
reporting per-class accuracies of 90--98\% across three diseases and two
pest-damage categories -- lower and more variable than lab-image results,
consistent with field conditions being harder. Brahimi et al.\ (2017)
\cite{brahimi2017} reached 99.18\% on nine tomato diseases and additionally
used visualization to localize the leaf regions driving each prediction, an
explainability goal this project pursues with Grad-CAM (Section~4.8). Amara
et al.\ (2017) \cite{amara2017} applied a CNN to banana leaf disease
classification, illustrating that these methods generalize across very
different crop morphologies, not only the tomato/potato/pepper family
studied most often.

Barbedo (2018) \cite{barbedo2018} reviewed the practical factors limiting
deep-learning-based plant disease recognition and identified dataset
realism -- controlled backgrounds, single centred leaves, uniform lighting --
as a central weakness of the PlantVillage-style benchmarks that most reported
accuracies (including the ones above) are measured on. Singh et al.\ (2020)
\cite{singh2020plantdoc} acted directly on this finding, introducing
PlantDoc, a 2{,}598-image dataset scraped and hand-annotated from real-world,
internet-sourced photographs across 13 species, specifically because models
trained purely on PlantVillage do not transfer well to such images. This
lab-to-field generalization gap is the single most important limitation
carried into this project's own results and is discussed explicitly in
Section~7.1 rather than left implicit.

Two further methodological threads inform this project's evaluation
protocol. Guo et al.\ (2017) \cite{guo2017calibration} showed that modern,
high-capacity neural networks tend to be poorly calibrated -- confident even
when wrong -- motivating this project's use of Expected Calibration Error
(Section~4.7) rather than accuracy alone to judge whether the model's
confidence score can be trusted by a downstream user. Selvaraju et al.\
(2017) \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} introduced Grad-CAM, a gradient-based
method for visualizing which image regions drive a CNN's prediction, used
here (Section~4.8) to check that the classifier attends to diseased leaf
tissue rather than to background artefacts. Shorten and Khoshgoftaar (2019)
\cite{shorten2019augmentation} surveyed image data-augmentation techniques
for deep learning, several of which (random flips, rotation, zoom,
translation, contrast/brightness jitter) are applied to the training pipeline
in Section~4.3 to reduce overfitting on a moderately imbalanced,
comparatively small dataset.

Finally, a diagnosis is only the first half of what a farmer needs. Singh
et al.\ (2024) \cite{singh2024farmerchat}, in Farmer.Chat, describe a
large-language-model-based conversational assistant that gives smallholder
farmers personalized, real-time agricultural advice, and note that
off-the-shelf language models tend to produce generic advice that is not
grounded in the farmer's actual context. This project takes a narrower but
more tightly grounded approach to the same underlying problem: rather than an
open-ended chat interface, a language model is invoked only after a specific
disease has been classified, and is conditioned explicitly on the predicted
crop, condition, computed severity, resolved location, and live weather
(Section~4.9), which constrains its output to a specific, actionable
recommendation instead of open-domain advice.

\subsection{System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the system's architecture. A Streamlit
web application is the single point of contact with the user and fans out to
five independent engines: image classification (local computation, using the
model developed in Sections~4.4--4.6), severity scoring (local, rule-based),
and three thin clients to external services -- a large-language-model API
for treatment-plan generation, a geocoding/weather pair for location context,
and a text-to-speech service for audio narration. No engine depends on
another directly; all are orchestrated through the web application's session
state, which keeps the design easy to extend with further engines later.

\subsection{Dataset}
The classification model is trained on a 15-class subset of the
\textbf{PlantVillage} dataset \cite{hughes2015}, spanning Pepper (bell),
Potato, and Tomato. The subset contains 20{,}638 indexed leaf images; every
file was verified with a full image-integrity pass (0 unreadable files), and
an exact-duplicate audit removed 14 duplicate files (none crossing class
boundaries), leaving \textbf{20{,}624 unique images} (Table~\ref{tab:classdist}).
The largest class is 21.1$\times$ the size of the smallest, a moderate
imbalance addressed in training (Section~4.6).

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Augmentation}
Images are resized to $224\times224\times3$ through a streaming input
pipeline that emits raw \texttt{[0,255]} pixel tensors; normalization is kept
inside each model rather than in the pipeline, so an exported model is
self-contained. Training images only are augmented on the fly with random
horizontal/vertical flips, rotation ($\pm20^{\circ}$), zoom (15\%),
translation (8\%), and contrast/brightness jitter (10--15\%), following the
general augmentation strategy surveyed by Shorten and Khoshgoftaar (2019)
\cite{shorten2019augmentation} for reducing overfitting on limited training
data. With a batch size of 32, this produces 452 training, 97 validation, and
97 test batches per epoch.

\subsection{Train/Validation/Test Split}
PlantVillage ships as one folder per class with no official split, so a
\textbf{stratified} 70/15/15 split (Table~\ref{tab:split}) was constructed so
every class keeps its proportion across all three sets, with an explicit
check confirming zero file overlap between splits. The test set was touched
exactly once, at the very end, and was not used for any decision (model
selection, early stopping, threshold tuning) during development.

\subsection{Model Architectures}
Two architectures were trained and compared under an identical protocol.

\subsubsection{Model A -- Custom CNN}
A VGG-style network \cite{simonyan2015}: six blocks of two $3\times3$
convolutions followed by batch normalization and $2\times2$ max-pooling, with
channel widths $64{\to}128{\to}128{\to}256{\to}512{\to}512$, followed by a
dense classifier head (Table~\ref{tab:cnnarch}), totalling 10.90M parameters.
Each output logit $z_k$ for class $k$ is converted to a class probability by
the softmax function,
\begin{equation}
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}, \qquad k = 1,\dots,C,
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
where $C=15$ is the number of classes. The network is trained by minimizing
categorical cross-entropy with label smoothing $\epsilon=0.05$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{k=1}^{C} \tilde{y}_k \log p_k, \qquad
\tilde{y}_k = (1-\epsilon)\,y_k + \frac{\epsilon}{C},
\label{eq:ce}
\end{equation}
where $y_k$ is the one-hot ground-truth label; label smoothing softens the
target distribution slightly to discourage over-confident predictions.

\subsubsection{Model B -- EfficientNetB3 (Transfer Learning)}
An ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNetB3 backbone \cite{tan2019efficientnet}
(global max pooling, top layer removed) with a lightweight head
(Table~\ref{tab:effnetarch}, 11.18M parameters total), trained in two stages
following standard transfer-learning practice \cite{pan2010transfer}: first
with the backbone entirely frozen (head-only training), then with the top 60
backbone layers unfrozen for fine-tuning at a reduced learning rate, while
batch-normalization layers are kept frozen throughout to preserve their
pretrained statistics.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
Both models were optimized with Adamax, a variant of the Adam optimizer
\cite{kingma2015adam} that replaces the second-moment estimate with an
infinity-norm-based term, which the original authors found more stable for
some embedding- and image-classification settings. Training used early
stopping (patience 6 epochs, restoring the best weights), a
learning-rate-on-plateau schedule (halving on stagnation), and class weights
to counter the moderate class imbalance (Section~4.2). Key hyperparameters
are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Accuracy alone is a misleading summary on an imbalanced dataset, so the
following metrics were computed on the held-out test set, with true/false
positives/negatives denoted $TP,FP,TN,FN$ (per class, then macro-averaged
where applicable):
\begin{align}
\text{Precision} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \label{eq:precision}\\
\text{Recall} &= \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \label{eq:recall}\\
F_1 &= \frac{2\cdot\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}, \label{eq:f1}\\
\text{Balanced Accuracy} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{k=1}^{C} \text{Recall}_k. \label{eq:balacc}
\end{align}
Cohen's kappa $\kappa$ corrects raw agreement $p_o$ (i.e.\ accuracy) for the
agreement $p_e$ expected by chance given the class marginals,
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}.
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), in its binary form generalized to
the multi-class setting following Gorodkin (2004) \cite{gorodkin2004mcc},
\begin{equation}
\text{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}},
\label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}
is a chance-corrected correlation between predicted and true labels that
remains informative under class imbalance. Finally, confidence calibration
was measured with Expected Calibration Error \cite{guo2017calibration},
which bins predictions by confidence and averages the gap between confidence
and empirical accuracy within each bin $b$ (of $B$ bins, $n_b$ predictions in
bin $b$, $N$ predictions total):
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{N} \left| \text{acc}(b) - \text{conf}(b) \right|.
\label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
All metrics were computed with scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}.

\subsection{Explainability}
Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} was used to generate class-discriminative
activation maps for the trained classifier's predictions, to check
qualitatively whether the model attends to diseased leaf tissue rather than
to background or framing artefacts (Section~6.9 and Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam}).

\subsection{Deployment Pipeline}
The selected model was exported to a quantized on-device format (both fp32
and fp16 variants) for lightweight inference. Every exported artefact was
re-run on real test images and its predictions compared against the
original model's, so that fidelity is verified rather than assumed
(Section~6.10). Float16 quantization was used because it roughly halves file
size with, in this project's own measurements, no additional accuracy loss
beyond fp32.

\subsection{Application Modules}
Around the classifier, five further modules complete the farmer-facing
system (architecture in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The
\textit{severity engine} maps a predicted condition to a severity level using
a lookup table of plant-pathology facts -- pathogen type, curability, spread
rate, and yield impact -- rather than the model's confidence score, so the
same diagnosis always receives the same severity regardless of how confident
a particular prediction happened to be. The \textit{treatment engine} calls a
hosted large language model, conditioned on the predicted crop, condition,
computed severity, resolved location, and current weather, to generate a
region-appropriate treatment plan -- the narrower, classification-triggered
counterpart to open-ended agricultural chat assistants such as Farmer.Chat
\cite{singh2024farmerchat} (Section~3). The \textit{location and weather
engine} resolves the farmer's location from either browser geolocation or a
typed place name, and fetches a short-range weather summary to condition the
treatment plan on real, current conditions. The \textit{speech engine}
converts the diagnosis summary and treatment plan to spoken audio in the
selected language. The \textit{multilingual interface} drives every
user-facing string from a single translation table, toggled between Hindi
and English at runtime.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Lab-to-Field Generalization Gap}
The 95.51\% test accuracy reported in Section~6.4 is, like the comparable
figures reported by Mohanty et al.\ (2016) \cite{mohanty2016} (99.35\%),
Ferentinos (2018) \cite{ferentinos2018} (99.53\%), and Too et al.\ (2019)
\cite{too2019} (99.75\%), a \textit{PlantVillage} number. PlantVillage images
share a near-constant, centred-leaf-on-grey-background composition
(Section~6.1, Figure~\ref{fig:meanimage}), which is convenient for training
but is also exactly the property Barbedo (2018) \cite{barbedo2018} identifies
as limiting real-world transfer, and the reason Singh et al.\ (2020)
\cite{singh2020plantdoc} built PlantDoc from field-scraped photographs in the
first place. This project's own result is not evidence that the deployed
model performs equally well on a farmer's actual photograph, and this report
does not claim otherwise; Section~7.4 gives the concrete steps needed before
that claim could be made.

\subsection{Why the Simpler Model Won}
The from-scratch Custom CNN outperforming EfficientNetB3 transfer learning
on this dataset (Table~\ref{tab:results}) runs somewhat against the general
trend in Too et al.\ (2019) \cite{too2019}, where deeper, ImageNet-pretrained
architectures were strongest. A plausible reconciliation is that
PlantVillage's controlled, lab-uniform imagery reduces the advantage
ImageNet-pretrained low-level features normally provide on natural-image
photographs, while this project's EfficientNetB3 was fine-tuned for only 7
epochs on the top 60 of 386 backbone layers (Section~4.6), a much lighter
fine-tuning budget than a from-scratch six-block CNN trained for 25 epochs
end-to-end. This is a specific, checkable hypothesis for follow-up work
(deeper or longer fine-tuning), not a claim that transfer learning is
generally weaker here.

\subsection{Calibration and the Case for Abstention}
The 0.936-vs-0.664 confidence gap between correct and incorrect predictions
(Section~6.6) is large enough to support a concrete design change not yet
implemented in the deployed application: routing predictions below a chosen
confidence threshold to a ``diagnosis unclear -- try another photo'' state
rather than presenting a possibly wrong label as if it were certain. Guo
et al.\ (2017) \cite{guo2017calibration} note that well-calibrated confidence
scores are precisely what make such thresholds trustworthy, which is why
Expected Calibration Error, not accuracy, was checked before proposing this.

\subsection{Deployment Trade-off}
Table~\ref{tab:tflite} shows EfficientNetB3 running over 4$\times$ faster on
CPU than the Custom CNN despite a similar parameter count, because its
architecture relies on cheaper depthwise-separable convolutions
\cite{tan2019efficientnet} rather than the Custom CNN's large dense
$3\times3$ convolutions applied at full $224\times224$ resolution in its
early layers. A latency-constrained deployment (e.g.\ a low-power mobile
device) could reasonably trade some of the Custom CNN's accuracy advantage
for EfficientNetB3's speed; the current system's choice favors accuracy
because inference in this application runs server-side, not on the farmer's
device.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Beyond the generalization gap (Section~7.1), the system covers 15 classes
across three crops and assumes one dominant condition per uploaded leaf
image; it does not detect multiple co-occurring diseases or aggregate a
whole-field assessment. The treatment, weather, and geocoding features
require network connectivity and depend on third-party API availability. The
application does not yet implement the confidence-based abstention discussed
in Section~7.2. Future work should prioritize, in order: (i)~evaluating and
fine-tuning against a field-collected set such as PlantDoc
\cite{singh2020plantdoc}, and reporting that number as the deployment-ready
metric; (ii)~implementing confidence-based abstention; (iii)~broadening class
and crop coverage; and (iv)~an offline-capable mobile client built directly
on the exported fp16 model (Section~6.10), avoiding a network round-trip for
the classification step.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================================
This project built a complete decision-support pipeline for farmers --
diagnosis, severity, and a localized, weather-aware treatment plan, delivered
bilingually with audio support -- rather than a bare classifier. Two
architectures were trained and compared under an identical, leakage-checked
protocol; the simpler, from-scratch CNN outperformed EfficientNetB3 transfer
learning on this dataset (95.51\% vs.\ 87.10\% test accuracy) while also
being better calibrated, and was exported to a quantized on-device format
with verified, near-lossless fidelity. Around this classifier, the system
reasons about severity from disease facts rather than model confidence, and
produces treatment advice grounded in the farmer's location and current
weather -- output substantially more actionable than a class label alone.
The report has also tried to state plainly what these results do and do not
show: the headline accuracy is a PlantVillage number, and the literature
reviewed in Section~3 is clear that such numbers do not transfer directly to
field conditions. Closing that gap, via the concrete steps in Section~7.5, is
the natural next phase of this work.
